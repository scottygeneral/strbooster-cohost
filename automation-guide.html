Practical Guide

Choose your first automation with confidence

By David Anthony Scott, founder of STR Booster · Updated November 2026

Start with a repetitive workflow that has a clear trigger and an observable outcome.

A solo host can now do much of the research, follow-up and analysis that once needed several specialists. AI helps you think and draft. Automation handles repeatable work. Neither can clean a room or open a jammed door, but together they can stop the repeated digital tasks that pull you away from higher-value decisions.

The right first automation is not the most impressive one. It is a repetitive workflow with a clear trigger and an outcome you can observe. This guide helps you choose it, build it safely and decide whether it is worth keeping.

1. Map the current task

Write down what starts the task, the information it needs, the steps involved and who is responsible. Identify exceptions before choosing a tool.

Start by finding where your time actually goes. For two ordinary weeks, log time spent on guest communication, turnovers, maintenance coordination, marketing, administration and travel, including interruptions. You are looking for patterns, not a perfect time study. The best candidates usually show up quickly: the same arrival question answered five times, every email written from scratch, the calendar checked by hand.

A written procedure, an SOP, is simply your way of doing a recurring task. A useful one fits on a phone screen and says what to check and what to do if something fails. If you cannot write the task down clearly, it is not ready to automate.

2. Use what you already have

Check your PMS, CRM and existing integrations for relevant features. Avoid adding a subscription that duplicates something your current system can do.

Many hosts already pay for scheduled messaging, saved replies and calendar sync they never switched on. Before buying anything, list the tools you use and what each can trigger: a confirmed booking, a changed date, a cancellation, a form submission. A CRM is simply the system that remembers your guest relationships; if yours can tag guests and send timed messages, your first workflow may need nothing new.

When you do need a connected sales and marketing system, choose one that covers several needs at once rather than stacking single-purpose apps.

3. Define the boundaries

Agree what the automation can change, when it must pause, and how it should escalate an exception. Keep human review where a mistake would materially affect a guest or booking.

Automation can bring a problem to your attention; it should not make promises you have not approved. An automated reply can acknowledge an urgent message and alert you, but it must not promise a refund or a repair time. If a guest replies to a sequence, pause any message that would ignore what they said.

Send each message for a reason the guest will recognise. Sending something at every stage because the software allows it overwhelms people. Combine related information, put access details in a controlled channel at the right time, and set response expectations you can actually meet.

4. Test a complete journey

Use test records to check triggers, timing, duplicate messages and failure handling. Confirm that the right person can see when a workflow fails.

For anything tied to a reservation, test three paths before you rely on it: a confirmed stay schedules the message; a changed arrival date moves it; a cancellation stops it. Most automation failures in rentals come from the second and third paths, where a guest receives check-in instructions for a stay that moved or no longer exists.

Also test what happens when something goes wrong: a missing phone number, a booking made at the last minute, a message that fails to send. Someone should be notified, not left to discover the problem from a guest.

5. Review the result

Compare the manual effort removed with the effort required to monitor and maintain the workflow. Keep the automation only if it improves the overall process.

Give your time a planning value, even though it is not a payroll expense. If a change produces $80 more contribution but takes four extra hours, that work returns $20 an hour before other considerations. Apply the same arithmetic to automation: hours saved each month, minus hours spent fixing and checking it, against what the tool costs.

Review after a month of real use. Look at guest questions, incidents and replies. If people still ask the question your message was meant to answer, rewrite the message or simplify the underlying process.

Worked example: the pre-arrival message

Illustrative. A cottage owner answers the same questions before every stay: where to park, how the steps affect luggage, how entry works and what the kitchen provides. The owner writes one concise arrival message with what guests need while travelling, keeps a fuller guide for later reading, and schedules the message from the confirmed reservation. Entry details go through the booking channel at the intended time. The three paths (confirm, change, cancel) are tested with test bookings, and a short post-arrival note offers help without demanding a reply: "If anything in the cottage needs attention, contact us through your usual booking channel. There is no need to reply if everything is fine."

The result is not a robot host. It is a consistent first evening and an inbox with fewer repeat questions, so the owner's attention goes to the exceptions that need a person.

Good first automations for rental owners

  • Booking confirmation with next steps.
  • Pre-arrival message with parking, access and first-evening information.
  • Post-arrival check-in that does not require a reply.
  • Departure reminder that is short and consistent with your terms.
  • Enquiry follow-up for people who asked but did not book, with their permission.
  • An alert to you when an urgent guest message arrives.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What should I automate first in my rental business?

Choose a repetitive task with a clear trigger and an outcome you can observe, such as a pre-arrival message or enquiry follow-up.

How do I avoid automation mistakes with guests?

Define what the automation may change, when it must pause and who it escalates to. Test the full journey with test records before switching it on.

Do I need to buy an automation tool?

Check your PMS, CRM and existing integrations first. Many have the features you need, so a new subscription may duplicate what you already pay for.
