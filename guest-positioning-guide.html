Practical Guide

Position your rental around the right guest

By David Anthony Scott, founder of STR Booster · Updated November 2026

A practical starting point for identifying who your property serves best.

One property can mean very different things to different guests. For the person organising a family celebration, it is the place that finally gets everyone under one roof. For someone planning a small retreat, the same shared rooms offer space to think without everyday interruptions. Your positioning explains why your stay is a sensible choice for a particular person on a particular occasion.

The aim of this guide is a one-page ideal guest profile that changes real decisions: which photo leads, what the opening paragraph says, what you explain before arrival and which quiet dates you test. If a profile changes nothing, it is only a description.

1. Start with the travel need

Describe why someone travels, who travels with them, and what a successful stay looks like. A purpose such as a quiet anniversary break is more useful than an age group alone.

"Couples", "families" and "business travellers" are starting points, not answers. Two couples booking the same town for the same weekend can want completely different things. One is celebrating an anniversary and plans to spend most of the weekend at the property; they care about privacy and a comfortable place to linger. The other is attending a wedding and needs mirrors, parking, an accurate travel time to the venue and a good night's sleep. Same party size, different reasons to choose.

Ask four questions about the trip:

  • What is bringing someone to your area?
  • What are they trying to make happen?
  • What could make the stay difficult?
  • What must be true for your property to work for them?

A useful profile connects the practical task to the emotional outcome. A family may want enough real beds and an easy meal routine because they want a holiday that feels less exhausting. The emotional benefit becomes believable when the property supports it in practical terms.

2. Check the evidence

Look for repeated experiences in guest reviews and recurring questions in enquiries. Separate consistent strengths from occasional perks, and record where the property is a poor fit.

Begin with what you already hold: booking records, reviews, enquiries and guest conversations, ideally across a full seasonal cycle. Booking records show when guests stayed, for how long, through which channel and at what price. Reviews show what guests noticed and the words they used. Enquiries expose the information people needed before deciding.

Keep observations separate from interpretations. "The guest asked whether the garden was enclosed" is an observation. "Dog owners are our strongest market" is an interpretation that needs more support. Read for repetition, but also for disagreement: several guests praising the beds identifies a dependable strength, while one guest delighted by a surprise anniversary gift does not create a standard inclusion to advertise.

A valuable guest is someone whose needs fit what you provide and whose booking supports the business you want to run. Spending is part of that, not all of it. A high nightly rate can bring high acquisition costs or a short gap that is hard to resell; a longer stay at a lower rate can perform well in a quiet period.

3. Choose the strongest promise

Connect a guest priority to something your property can reliably deliver. Make the promise specific and support it with accurate photos, amenities and location detail.

Guests rarely choose a property in isolation. They compare it with other rentals, hotels, a different destination or postponing the trip. Pick three to five realistic alternatives for your priority trip and compare the same dates and party size: total price shown, sleeping arrangements, location advantages, limitations and cancellation terms. You do not need to claim nobody else has a deck. You need the combination of your offer to be clear enough that the right guest recognises it.

Write three short chains of property fact → guest use → supporting proof. The cottage has a kitchen; for two adults on a restful break, that means cooking and staying in for the evening; the proof is an equipment list and accurate photos. If a chain breaks, you need better information, an operational improvement or a more modest claim.

Your promise must stay true on an ordinary Tuesday, in poor weather, when you are not personally present. Positioning should also help you decline investments: an amenity earns its place when it improves guest fit and has a commercial case, not because every listing nearby has one.

4. Apply it across the listing

Use the title and first photos to establish the experience. Expand the reasons to choose the stay in your description and answer practical concerns before they interrupt a booking.

Give every photo a job. The opening image should show a distinctive, usable part of the stay. The next images should establish the living space, the bedrooms and beds, the kitchen, the bathroom and access. Put important limitations, such as outdoor steps, where guests will see them during a normal scroll, not after a dozen decorative close-ups. Use captions where they remove doubt: "Private deck for cottage guests", "Access to the entrance includes outdoor steps".

Write the facts before the flourish. Compare these two openings for the same property:

Weak: "Welcome to our stunning luxury escape, perfect for everyone, with every amenity you could need."
Stronger: "Settle into a two-bedroom woodland cottage with a private deck, a kitchen for evenings in, and parking at the property. It sleeps up to four. Access includes outdoor steps, and the garden is not enclosed."

The second version gives a place, capacity, useful features, a possible experience and the limitations that matter. Clear limitations make the positive claims more credible.

5. Review by season

Different weeks can attract different travel needs. Revisit the guest profile when the reason to travel changes, while keeping the property facts consistent.

A property can keep one identity while serving different trips through the year: families during school holidays, couples' short breaks in quieter weeks. Start with the dates that actually need support. "More couples" is too broad if summer weekends already sell; "test demand for two-adult breaks on selected autumn weekdays" gives the work a commercial purpose.

Then test without pretending to prove too much. Write down the audience, dates, message and change, and record a baseline. Avoid changing photos, rates, minimum stays and the message all at once if you want to learn which change helped. Suitable bookings support the direction; a handful do not establish a rule.

Worked example: a woodland cottage

This cottage and its figures are illustrative. A two-bedroom cottage sleeps four, has a private deck, a usable kitchen and parking. Access includes outdoor steps and the garden is not enclosed. The owner reviews 30 completed stays: 12 couples' weekend breaks, 10 family holidays, 4 wedding stays and 4 with no recorded purpose. Seven couples mention quiet or privacy and five mention the deck. Families appreciate the kitchen but ask about the stairs and the garden boundary.

The resulting profile: two adults on a short driving break, mainly to rest at the property. Evidence is strongest for weekends; midweek demand is unproven. The owner shows the deck and kitchen clearly while disclosing the steps, improves first-evening arrival information, and tests a quiet-break message for selected autumn weekdays before changing anything bigger.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Building the profile from demographics instead of the trip.
  • Treating one enthusiastic review as a market.
  • Promising outcomes ("you will reconnect") instead of describing conditions the property provides.
  • Hiding limitations on a policy page that guests never read.
  • Redesigning the whole property around an imagined guest before testing demand.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What is an ideal guest for a vacation rental?

An ideal guest is the type of traveller your property serves best, defined by why they travel, who they travel with and what a successful stay looks like for them, rather than by age group alone.

Where do I find evidence of my best guest?

Start with your guest reviews and the questions people ask in enquiries. Repeated praise points to real strengths; repeated concerns show where the property is a poor fit.

How often should I review my guest positioning?

Review it whenever the main reason to travel changes, typically each season, while keeping the property facts consistent.
