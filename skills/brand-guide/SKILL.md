# Brand Guide

Step 2 of the free STR Booster playbook (Brand building). It answers one question: **why does a guest pick this place over the cheaper one down the road, and book it direct next time?** Then it turns the answer into rules anyone can use: a cleaner writing a card, a co-host answering a message, a photographer, a copywriter, or Claude writing a listing.

The output is:
1. A styled HTML/PDF **Brand Guide** in the property's own colors.
2. A short **`brand.md`** with the brand core. The later skills read it: the OTA Distribution plan, the Monthly Marketing Plan and the Guest Experience Storyboard all start from the promise, the voice and the banned words in this file.

Use it when someone asks to build a brand for their rental, write a brand guide or brand voice, work out how to position a property, stand out from other listings, stop competing on price, define a brand for a property management company, or "start the brand step".

The person is usually a busy host or property manager with thin margins and too many apps. Don't recommend new software, agencies or paid tools. Everything here is Claude plus what they already have.

## Three ideas behind the method

**1. The brand is a decision filter, not a decoration.** The guide must be specific enough to settle a hard call: which photo leads the listing, whether to allow events, what the welcome card says, whether to discount. If it can't settle a hard call, it isn't finished.

**2. Diagnose first, then write. Never show the diagnosis.** Run the five-part diagnosis below to find the positioning. The answers go into the guide as finished statements. The guide never shows framework labels, worksheets or "fill this in" boxes.

**3. The words you ban define you as much as the words you use.** Every guide has an explicit banned-words list. The usual offenders in rental copy: stunning, breathtaking, luxurious, cozy, charming, nestled, boasting, oasis, hidden gem, incredible, amazing, magical, and exclamation marks. If any of them appear in the guide itself, start that section again.

## Step 1: Gather what already exists

Look before you ask. Read, in this order:

| Input | Where it comes from | What you take |
|---|---|---|
| Goals, properties and rules | `business-brain.md` from **The Brain** skill (step 1) | Who the business serves, the 12-month goal, the rules it never breaks, the voice notes. |
| The listing | Airbnb, Vrbo or website URL | Facts: size, layout, setting, amenities, house rules, photos, the current title and description. |
| Reviews | Pasted reviews, or the listing's review section | The exact words happy guests use. These are the best source for the promise and the voice. |
| Ideal guest | The **Guest Intelligence Report** (step 3), if it already exists | Who books, why, and what they fear. Optional here: step 2 drafts the guest, step 3 deepens it. |
| Logo or colors | An uploaded logo image, a website, or hex codes | The real palette. |

Then ask only what is still missing, **one question at a time**, in plain words. Stop asking once you have enough. The short set:

1. "In a sentence or two, what is the place and where is it?" (Skip if you have the listing.)
2. "Is this one property, a few, or a management company with a portfolio?"
3. "Does it run more than one kind of business? For example stays plus weddings, retreats or mid-term."
4. "If a happy guest described it to a friend in a few words, what would you want them to say?" Offer anchors if they are stuck: quiet, discovered, together, easy, local, unhurried, a proper treat.
5. "Who do you lose bookings to? Name places, or types: hotels, other rentals, staying home."
6. "Who should not book it?" (This sharpens the promise faster than any other question.)
7. "If the place were a person at a dinner party, how would they dress, how much would they talk, and what would they never say?" Then: "What word would they never use to describe the view?"
8. "Do you have a logo? Please upload it." Without one: "Which of these is closest: natural and grounded, clean and modern, warm and classic, or bold and playful?"

Watch for these early and raise them before writing:
- **Two audiences on one channel.** For example, weddings and family stays on the same Airbnb listing. The architecture decision in Step 3 comes first.
- **"We want to be luxury."** Luxury is a price point, not a position. Push for the specific pairing: luxury plus quiet, plus land, plus design, plus service.
- **"Guests love it."** Press for the before and after. What do reviews say surprised them?
- **A logo that exists but wasn't shared.** Ask for it. A palette built without it will fight the identity they already have.

## Step 2: The diagnosis (internal, never shown)

Work through five questions. Write the answers in your notes, not in the guide.

| Question | What to pin down | Where it shows up in the guide |
|---|---|---|
| **Who exactly?** | The guest's life stage, the trigger for the trip, the budget mindset, what they are getting away from. Not a demographic. | Who it is for, every voice example |
| **What changes for them?** | How they feel arriving versus leaving. | The promise, the mission |
| **What worry does it remove?** | The doubts and pain points the place solves without saying so. | Competitive position, voice examples |
| **Why believe it?** | Verifiable specifics: acreage, minutes to the venue, ratings, review lines, the host's record. | Proof, voice examples |
| **What can't be copied?** | The combination no competitor has: location plus layout plus host plus story. | Positioning statement, the moat, the landscape table |

For a **portfolio or management company**, run the diagnosis once for the company (who the owners and guests are, what the company always does) and once per property type or group. The company brand is the master; each property gets an expression (Step 3).

## Step 3: Decide the brand architecture

This is the most consequential decision in the guide. Choose one and write it in one line with the reason:

| Situation | Model | The rule |
|---|---|---|
| One property, one kind of guest | **One brand** | Everything carries the same name, promise and voice. Name the trigger that would change this (a second unit, events, a new market). |
| One property, two businesses (stays plus events, retreats or mid-term) | **One master brand, two expressions** | The master owns the name, values, look and story. Each expression has its own audience, channels, language and booking path. They share the look, never the channels or the messages. |
| A few properties that serve the same guest | **One brand, named homes** | One promise across the group. Each home has a name and one line of its own, but the voice and rules are shared. |
| A management company with a portfolio | **Company brand, property expressions** | The company brand speaks to owners and repeat guests (the website, the direct booking page, owner reports). Each property, or group of similar properties, gets an expression with its own promise for its own guest. Guests should feel the company's standards, not see a logo on every page. |

Rules that hold in every model:
- **Expressions never share channels or messages.** Stay language never appears in events channels. Events language never appears on OTA listings.
- **A separate sub-brand name is rarely worth it at first.** "Name, Private Events" or "Name, Monthly Stays" usually does the job. Flag when to revisit: when the second business passes about 40% of revenue, or gets its own team.
- **Events and high-touch services sell direct.** A couple or a planner who finds a venue through a vacation rental listing gets the wrong first signal. Direct enquiry only.

## Step 4: Write the promise

The promise is the hardest line and the most important. Write five to eight candidates, then test each one:

1. **Is it true of this place and false of the cheaper alternative?** The motel down the road, the tent, the downtown condo. If the cheaper option could also keep this promise, sharpen it. "A peaceful getaway" fails: every rental says it. "Eleven acres between you and noise" passes: it names a fact only this place has.
2. **Does it reframe the choice,** rather than list a feature?
3. **Does it hold for every expression?** For two-business properties, it must be true for the overnight guest and the events client.
4. **Is it six words or fewer?** Discipline reads as confidence.

Patterns that work:
- **Subtraction:** what the place removes (noise, planning, the commute, the crowd). Luxury as the absence of clutter.
- **The place does the work:** not "we offer access to the lake" but the lake itself as the active force.
- **The frame:** the home makes itself worthy of what it holds, then gets out of the way.
- **One earned word:** a single word that carries the quality argument without stating it, such as considered, deliberate, earned, kept.

Then write the **promise meaning**: one plain sentence that turns the promise into a rule for decisions. Example: "The quiet is the product. Every touchpoint protects it or gets out of its way."

Show the person your top three candidates with one line each on why, recommend one, and let them pick.

## Step 5: Write the rest of the identity

- **Mission**: testable, specific to the guest's condition, no clichés ("memorable experiences", "above and beyond", "home away from home" are banned). Shape: "To create [specific condition] so that [specific outcome], by [what makes it possible]." Write it fresh for this place.
- **Values**: four single words, each with what it means in practice at this property ("Quiet: no check-in visits, no noisy appliances, no events").
- **Personality**: one sentence ("a calm neighbor who answers fast and then leaves you be").
- **Name**: what the name means, and the usage rules (always written the same way, never abbreviated, how co-hosts and cleaners refer to it).
- **Who it is for**: the specific guest in one or two sentences, the trigger for their trip, and the before and after. **Who it is not for**: two to four kinds of guest, said kindly but plainly. Saying no is part of the brand.

## Step 6: Write the competitive position

- **Positioning statement**: two sentences at most. Specific, checkable, no superlatives. Test: could any other property in the market make the same claim? If yes, make it more specific. Five shapes that work:
  1. *Place plus home*: "[Acreage or setting] [location context]. A home built to put nothing between you and [the specific thing]."
  2. *Subtraction*: "The [property type] that removes [the obstacle] instead of adding [what competitors add]."
  3. *The geography*: "[Specific geography]. [Layout fact]. [What that combination makes possible.]"
  4. *Promise form*: "[One sensory detail]. [What happens to a guest here.]"
  5. *Audience claim*: "For [specific guest] who [belief about travel]." (Best as a companion line, not alone.)
- **Proof**: three to five claims, each with the evidence (a review quote, a measured distance, a rating, a fact from the listing). No proof, no claim.
- **The moat**: two to four advantages and why each is hard to copy.
- **The landscape**: the three to five real alternatives the guest compares (named places or types), what each offers, and when the guest picks this place instead. Be fair to the competition. Never invent their prices or ratings; mark anything unconfirmed "Est.".

## Step 7: Write the voice

This is the part writers and Claude will use every day. Make it concrete enough to copy.

- **Three voice words**, each with what it sounds like and what it never sounds like. ("Plain: 'Twenty-five minutes to the stadium.' Never: 'Conveniently located near all the action.'")
- **Voice in practice**: a three-column table with 5 to 7 rows. Left: the context (the setting, the home, privacy, an evening, a problem, a call to action, a review reply). Middle: **actual copy** in the brand voice, not a description of copy. Use numbers, times of day, distances, names of real things. No adverbs. Right: **the actual bad version**, the full cliché sentence with the exclamation mark. Make it bad enough that anyone recognizes the type at once.
- **Words we use**: 5 to 8 words or phrases that belong to this brand (taken from reviews where possible).
- **Words we never use**: 6 to 10, always including the usual offenders that don't fit, plus anything the owner wants to move away from.
- **Voice by channel**: one line each for the OTA listing, guest messages, the house book and cards, social posts, email, and owner communication (for managers).

Voice starting points by property type. Use them as a start, then tune to this place:

| Type | Core tension the brand holds | Strong words | Weak words |
|---|---|---|---|
| Land, farm, mountain, wilderness | Wild versus comfortable. Hold both. | acres, ridge, morning, the light at, what the land does | breathtaking, escape, serene, nestled, surrounded by |
| Beach and lake | Easy versus looked after | steps to, tide, dock, sand in the car, the hour before dinner | paradise, oasis, beachy vibes, slice of heaven |
| City and downtown | Close to everything versus a calm base | blocks, minutes, the corner, the walk home, quiet street | heart of it all, vibrant, steps from everything |
| Design-led home | Artful versus comfortable. Design in service of the guest. | frame, line, material, light, considered, designed to | luxury finishes, top of the line, beautifully appointed |
| Heritage, historic, ranch | Old versus modern comfort, without apology | built in, generations, working, weathered, the history of | rustic charm, old-world, vibes, kitsch |
| Family and group homes | Together versus room to breathe | sleeps, long table, the bunk room, one roof, everyone | fun for the whole family, something for everyone |
| Management company (owner-facing) | Hands-on versus reliable systems | reported, checked, by Friday, your home, the numbers | full-service, hassle-free, maximize your ROI |

If the property's name carries a story (literature, history, a family name), let the name do its work. Don't explain it in every piece of copy, and never translate it into adjectives like magical or enchanting.

## Step 8: Visual direction

**Palette.** If there is a logo, use its real colors. Never invent a palette when a logo exists.
- If you can run code, sample the logo: load it with Python (Pillow), drop transparent and near-white pixels, quantize to 6 colors, and take the darkest cluster as **ink**, the logo's background or lightest warm tone as **ground**, the warmest secondary tone as **accent**, and a mid-dark cool tone as **support**. Check that ink on ground passes contrast (4.5:1).
- If you can't run code, look at the logo and estimate the colors, and say they are estimates the person should check against their designer's codes.
- No logo: choose four calm colors that fit the place and the guest, and say plainly that they are a starting point.

**Type.** A display face for headlines (Georgia is the safe default) and a plain sans-serif for body text. Labels in small capitals with wide letter spacing. No script fonts or rustic decorative faces.

**Photography.** What to shoot (specific shots that prove the promise, in the light of day it happens) and what to avoid (wide-angle distortion, empty staged rooms with no sign of life, filters, drone shots that hide the home, anything that breaks the promise).

## Step 9: Distribution alignment and the usage rules

- **Distribution**: for each expression, the channels it lives on, what it says there, and what it never says there. Then the separation rules as a short list. Keep it short: the OTA Distribution skill (step 5) does the full channel plan.
- **The decision filter**: four questions, in this order. Fill in the real promise and guest:
  1. Does it serve the right guest? (Name the specific guest, not "anyone who loves nature".)
  2. Does it keep the promise? (Quote the promise. Anything that adds noise at its expense is wrong.)
  3. Does it keep the expressions apart? (For one-brand properties: does it pull in the wrong guest?)
  4. Is it specific? (Give contrasting pairs from this brand: "eleven acres" over "private", "25 minutes" over "close to".)
- **Always**: 5 to 6 things the brand always does. **Never**: 5 to 6 things it never does.

## Step 11: Render

1. Save the template below as `brand-guide.html`.
2. Replace the `{}` inside `<script type="application/json" id="guide-data">` with your JSON. Change nothing else. If any string contains `</`, write it as `<\/`.
3. Make the PDF if you can run code: open the HTML in headless Chromium (Playwright `page.pdf({format:'Letter', printBackground:true})`, or `chrome --headless --print-to-pdf`). Otherwise deliver the HTML and tell the person to open it and use Print, then Save as PDF.
4. Look at the result before you deliver it. Check for empty sections, raw HTML and unreadable color pairs.
5. Name files `brand-guide-{property-slug}.html/.pdf`.

Tell the person to add `brand.md` (and the PDF) to the same Claude project as `business-brain.md`.

Deliver the PDF (or HTML) with a 3-line summary: the promise, the one guest it is for, and the one change to make first (usually the listing title or the first photo). Then point to what comes next: step 3, the Guest Intelligence Report, deepens the guest; step 4 rewrites the listing in this voice; the Guest Experience Storyboard (step 9) tests every touchpoint against this promise.

## Quality check before delivering

- [ ] The promise is six words or fewer and passes the cheaper-alternative test.
- [ ] The positioning statement is two sentences at most, with no superlatives.
- [ ] The mission names the specific guest condition, not a generic one.
- [ ] No diagnosis labels, worksheet prompts or "input needed" boxes appear anywhere.
- [ ] The banned-words list is present, and none of those words appear in the guide itself.
- [ ] Voice examples are real copy, not descriptions of copy, and the bad column is truly bad.
- [ ] Every proof claim has evidence. Nothing about the property, reviews or competitors is invented. Unknowns are marked "Est." or left as a bracketed placeholder.
- [ ] The palette comes from the logo when one exists, and `paletteSource` says how.
- [ ] Expressions are kept apart in the distribution section.
- [ ] The decision filter has four questions, in order, filled in for this brand.
- [ ] No urgency, no exclamation marks, no promotional third person ("This beautiful guide features...").
- [ ] Photography direction includes what to avoid.

## Rules

- Look before you ask, then ask one question at a time.
- Never invent listing facts, reviews, ratings, awards or competitor details. Use bracketed placeholders and say what is missing.
- Never invent a palette when a logo exists.
- The guide is finished work: written statements, not a framework or a worksheet.
- No new apps, agencies or subscriptions. No affiliate or referral links.
- Keep the brand honest. A promise the property can't keep every stay is worse than no promise.
- The guest is a composite. Don't use real guest names.
- Keep sentences short and concrete.
