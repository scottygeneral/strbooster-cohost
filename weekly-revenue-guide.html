Practical Guide

Connect your weekly pricing and marketing

By David Anthony Scott, founder of STR Booster · Updated November 2026

A simple planning routine for keeping demand, price and promotion aligned.

An empty night is an outcome. It does not tell you why the night stayed empty. Perhaps too few suitable people saw the property, the offer did not fit their trip, they wanted dates you had blocked, or a minimum stay made the gap impossible to book. These look identical on a calendar and need very different responses. A discount will not repair a restriction problem, and more advertising will not make an unavailable Friday available.

This guide gives you a weekly routine, one focused sitting, that keeps demand, price, stay rules and promotion working from the same plan.

Before you start: the numbers you will use

  • Sellable nights: calendar nights minus owner use and planned maintenance. Record those separately so blocking unsold nights never flatters your occupancy.
  • Occupancy: sold nights divided by sellable nights. Eighteen sold out of 24 sellable nights is 75%.
  • ADR (average daily rate): lodging revenue divided by sold nights, keeping cleaning fees separate.
  • Revenue per available night: lodging revenue divided by sellable nights. It combines rate and occupancy.
  • Booking contribution: what a reservation leaves after its own costs: channel or payment fees, acquisition cost, turnover and variable stay costs. It is not final profit, but it is the right number for comparing opportunities.

1. Review the booking position

Compare booked nights, lead time and pace for the weeks ahead. Separate owner-blocked nights from nights you genuinely intend to sell.

A booking-pace view looks at what is already on the books for future dates at the same point before arrival. Comparing next month's booked nights with last year's final total is premature; compare with what you had at the equivalent lead time, then allow for cancellations and changed conditions.

Inspect the calendar as a guest would. Search the trip lengths, arrival days and party sizes you hope to sell. A three-night minimum can leave an unsellable two-night gap; a restricted arrival day can block an otherwise open stay; an incorrect capacity setting can exclude a party you can host. These may be deliberate choices, but know their cost.

2. Identify the reason to travel

Consider the relevant guest, seasonal activities, events and booking window for each period. Verify event dates before building a campaign around them.

Every soft week should have a named guest and a reason they would come. An autumn weekday might suit two adults on a quiet break; a school-holiday week might suit a family organiser. Check feasibility: is there enough to do at that time of year, can guests reach the property easily, and does your minimum stay fit the trip? A persuasive message cannot fix an offer that is impractical for the dates being promoted.

Not every gap needs a new audience. Some are better handled through pricing, restrictions, maintenance scheduling, or accepting that opening those dates has a cost.

3. Choose the commercial response

Decide whether the gap calls for a pricing adjustment, a stay-rule change, clearer positioning or more relevant promotion. Account for the cost of acquiring the booking.

Match the response to the obstacle. Low impressions point to availability, eligibility or listing completeness. Plenty of views but few clicks point to the lead photo, title, displayed total price or the alternatives beside you. Visitors who check dates but cannot find a stay point to inventory and restrictions.

Then check the economics. Take an illustrative three-night stay worth $690 (lodging plus retained cleaning fee) with $130 of turnover and stay costs. Through a channel charging 15%, it contributes about $456. Booked direct from your own email list with a $30 discount, 3% payment fees and $10 of campaign cost, it contributes about $500. Booked direct through a paid ad costing $100 per booking, it contributes about $410, less than the channel booking. "Direct" is not automatically better; compare what each route leaves you.

A higher ADR on fewer nights is not automatically a better outcome, and neither is a full calendar at a low rate. Judge against contribution and the time the booking demands from you.

4. Coordinate the message

Match the offer and marketing theme to the target guest. Check that the message, availability and booking rules agree across channels.

The most common failure is inconsistency: a campaign promotes a two-night autumn break while the booking engine still requires three nights, or an old social post advertises dates that are no longer open. The guest sees a broken promise, whichever system caused it.

Before you send anything, confirm the promoted dates are open, the minimum stay matches the offer, the total price is clear, and the conditions are stated. Then lead with the trip, not the discount: "a quiet two-night break with the deck to yourselves" gives a reason to book that a percentage off does not.

5. Measure what happened

Review booking pickup and revenue alongside campaign activity. Treat observed changes as signals to investigate, rather than proof that one action caused the result.

Keep a small scorecard for one reporting period with one inventory definition: sellable and sold nights, lodging revenue, ADR, reservations, average stay length, cancellations and the channel behind each booking. For your website, add availability searches, checkout starts and completed reservations where you can track them reliably. Label every rate so someone else could reproduce it, and never compare an OTA conversion rate with a website rate as if they counted the same people.

Write a one-line note of what you changed each week. With small booking volumes, that change log is often more useful than a formal test, and it gives next month's comparison its context.

Your weekly routine on one page

  • Pull pace for the next 4–12 weeks and compare it with the same lead time last year.
  • Search your own calendar for the stays you want to sell; fix any restriction that blocks them.
  • Name the guest and reason to travel for each soft week.
  • Choose one response per week: price, stay rule, positioning or promotion, and check its contribution.
  • Confirm the message, dates and rules match on every channel.
  • Record what you changed and review pickup next week.

A useful AI prompt for the review: "Explain these booking figures in plain English. Separate what the data shows from possible explanations. Identify the three decisions I should consider, and tell me what information is missing." Always check the arithmetic against your dashboard.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

How often should I review vacation rental pricing?

A weekly review works well for most rentals: check booking pace and lead time for the weeks ahead, then decide whether price, stay rules or promotion need to change.

Should I lower my price when a week is not booking?

Not automatically. A slow week may need a stay-rule change, clearer positioning or more relevant promotion instead. Account for the cost of acquiring the booking before discounting.

How do I know if a pricing or marketing change worked?

Compare booking pickup and revenue with what you changed. Treat the result as a signal to investigate rather than proof that one action caused it.
